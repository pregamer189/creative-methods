# Emotion graphics

Our working meaning: **motion, sound and small characters that carry a feeling**,
so the product tells you how you're doing without words. It sits on top of
`../motion-graphics/ui-motion.md` (how things move) and answers a different
question: what should the user *feel* at this moment, and what makes them feel it?

Grounded in Elias's ideas I3 (premium = the felt satisfaction of the in-between
moments), I21 (tiny living details, a world that zooms), I27 (sound through
everything, special moments, easter eggs), I28 (complexity woven into simple
means), I30 (grand but never playful-looking), I31 (a tutor's short voice reactions; "sound" means a person's voice, Elias
2026-10-31),
2026-10-26; and in store reviews of course-style language apps (what users love
and hate, 2026-10).

## Rules

1. **Name the feeling first.** For each moment write one line: what happened, what
   the user should feel (proud, curious, calm, surprised), and the one cue that
   carries it (a motion, a sound, a character's reaction).
2. **One moment per screen.** Everything else stays quiet so the moment lands.
3. **Always ends upbeat.** Every outcome ends on a good sound and a kind motion,
   even a light or failed day. Low effort gets a smaller celebration, never a sad
   one. A character may give a brief, gentle "oops" to a miss: a short **human
   voice sound** (a real person's "oh", "mm-hm", a soft laugh; never a beep or a
   random effect, and never a robot voice) or a look, mostly positive and never discouraging, and it resolves into
   encouragement within the same moment (I31). The product's own rules can be
   stricter (e.g. "the streak object only ever sounds good").
4. **No guilt, no fear.** No crying or desperate mascots, no sad app icon, no
   "you'll lose it", no red alarms, no countdowns meant to scare. Store reviews
   call these "passive aggressive guilt trips" and a "nightly panic". Setbacks are
   shown honestly and calmly, with the way back visible.
5. **Change you can see.** Small animations that show progress or skill ("you're
   getting better") beat numbers. The product changes a little as the user grows.
6. **Tiny living details.** Small characters or objects that react to what the
   user does (a page settles when you save, something in the scene responds when
   you finish). They react to actions, they don't idle-loop for attention. Rendered
   close to real, never cartoonish.
7. **Rare things stay rare.** Special animations for real milestones (an even
   streak, a first, a new level) and a few easter eggs. If it happens every day it
   is not special: scale it down. Mix predictable rewards (every finish gets its
   moment) with controlled surprise (an occasional discovery, like Finch's bird
   finds), always guilt-free (Tim Gabe, "addictive apps", 2026).
8. **Sound through everything, quietly.** Whoosh, tick, chime, a world sound that
   fits the scene. Short, optimistic, never tiring; fully usable muted.
9. **Simple surface, rich underneath.** The delight comes from craft in small
   parts, not from adding screens or effects (I28).
10. **Close to real, never kid-like** (Elias, 2026-10-31: "starting to feel like a
    kids app, don't do that; quality should be close to real"). No avatars to dress
    up, no cartoon mascots, no toy-like figures. Characters stay undecided until the
    owner picks a direction; if used, they look close to real. Delight is crafted,
    calm and universal, not childish. Reviewers call heavy game layers "infantilising"; keep it to a brief
    moment and real craft (I30, reviews).
11. **Delight never costs task time.** A moment never blocks the next action and
    the world stays in the background of the work (I29, I30). Let users turn off
    extra animation and sound without losing anything else.
12. **Transitions can carry meaning.** The whole space may zoom or shift to show
    where the user is or how far they've come (I21), but only on a real change of
    place or level, never as decoration.
13. **Premium is the unglamorous parts too.** Empty states, errors, loading and the
    way a mistake is recovered get the same care; cohesion across screens matters
    more than one showpiece (Tim Gabe, "premium apps").
14. **Characters from a human hand.** A drawn base for each character, with AI used
    only for variations, keeps them from looking generated (Chris Raroque, app feel).
15. **Respect the user's settings.** Reduced motion keeps the feeling through sound
    and a still change; sound off keeps it through motion.

## Moment sheet (copy per moment)

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Finished today's session |
| Feeling | Proud, quietly delighted |
| Visual cue | The progress object moves up one step with a small spring |
| Sound | Rising three-note chime, under 600 ms |
| Haptic | Success notification |
| Scale by outcome | Great: full move + chime. Light day: half move + softer chime |
| Never | A discouraging sound, a loss animation, a guilt line, a sad icon |
| Reduced motion | Object swaps state, chime still plays |

## Checks

- Every moment has a filled sheet before it's built.
- Watch it at the worst outcome: is it still kind?
- Watch it ten days in a row (fast-forward): does it get old? If so, add variation
  or make it rarer.
- One outside eye: does a person who didn't build it say the feeling you named?
